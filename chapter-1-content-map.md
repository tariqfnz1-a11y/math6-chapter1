## 3. Mathematical Concepts & Definitions (Preserving Textbook Terminology)

1. **Factors (p. 1 / PDF p. 4):**
   - *"Factors are the numbers which we multiply to get another number say, product or total."*
   - *"A factor is a number which divides the dividend completely leaving no remainder."*
   - Division relation used: $\text{dividend} = \text{quotient} \times \text{divisor} + \text{remainder}$ (factor when remainder $= 0$).

2. **Multiples (p. 2 / PDF p. 5):**
   - *"A multiple is the product or total which we get when we multiply one number by another number."*
   - *"multiple of a given number is the dividend, for which the given number is its factor."*
   - Division relation: $\text{Dividend} \div \text{Divisor} = \text{Quotient}$ (with remainder `'0'`).

3. **Prime Numbers (p. 4 / PDF p. 7):**
   - *"A natural number which has only two different factors, 1 and the number itself, is called prime number."*
   - Denoted by capital letter $\text{P}$: $\text{P} = \{2, 3, 5, \dots\}$.

4. **Composite Numbers (p. 5 / PDF p. 8):**
   - *"A natural number which has more than two different factors is a composite number."*
   - Denoted by capital letter $\text{C}$: $\text{C} = \{4, 6, 8, 9, \dots\}$.

5. **Neither Prime Nor Composite (p. 6 / PDF p. 9):**
   - The number $1$ has only one factor (itself), so it is classified as **Neither Prime Nor-Composite**. *(See verification note regarding $0$ under Exercise 1.1 Q7.)*

6. **Twin Primes (p. 6 / PDF p. 9, Practice -4):**
   - *"Twin primes are prime numbers that differ by 2. Such as 3 and 5."*

7. **Factorization (p. 7 / PDF p. 10):**
   - *"The process of writing a given number into its factors is known as factorization."*

8. **Prime Factors & Prime Factorization (p. 7 / PDF p. 10):**
   - *"A composite number can be expressed as the product of two or more prime numbers, which are called prime factors."*
   - *"The process of factorizing a number into its prime factors is known as prime factorization."*

9. **Index Notation, Base, Index/Exponent, Expanded Form (p. 8 / PDF p. 11):**
   - *"The power (index or exponent) of a number says how many times to use the number in factorization."*
   - *"The representation of prime factors in the exponential form is called index notation."*
   - In $2^5$: $2$ is the **Base**, $5$ is the **Index** (power/exponent).
   - **Expanded form** vs **Index notation**: e.g., $2 \times 2 \times 3 \times 3 \times 3$ (expanded form) $= 2^2 \times 3^3$ (index notation).

10. **H.C.F — Highest Common Factor (p. 9 / PDF p. 12 & p. 10 / PDF p. 13):**
    - *"H.C.F of two or more numbers is a greatest or highest number which divides all the given numbers."*
    - *"H.C.F or highest common factor of two or more given numbers is the common factor of given numbers which is the greatest of all common factors."*
    - Also called **G.C.F** (Greatest Common Factor) or **G.C.D** (Greatest Common Divisor).

11. **Co-prime Numbers (p. 11 / PDF p. 14):**
    - *"If the H.C.F of two or more numbers is 1, then the numbers are called co-prime."*

12. **L.C.M — Least Common Multiple (p. 12 / PDF p. 15):**
    - *"L.C.M of two or more numbers is a least number which is divisible by all the given numbers."*
    - *"L.C.M or least common multiple of two or more numbers is the smallest of all their common multiples."*

13. **Relationship Between HCF and LCM (p. 15 / PDF p. 18):**
    - $\text{H.C.F.} \times \text{L.C.M.} = \text{First number} \times \text{Second number}$
    - $\text{L.C.M.} = \dfrac{\text{First number} \times \text{Second number}}{\text{H.C.F.}}$

14. **Square Numbers (p. 16 / PDF p. 19):**
    - *"Numbers or number of objects that can be arranged into a square array are known as square numbers."*
    - Calculated by multiplying a number (or integer) by itself: e.g., $(-3) \times (-3) = (-3)^2 = 9$ and $7 \times 7 = 7^2 = 49$.

## 5. Rules, Important Observations & Callout Boxes

### Fun Facts
- **p. 2 (PDF p. 5):**
  - All even numbers will have number 2 as their factor.
  - All numbers end with 5 will have 5 as their factor.
  - All numbers greater than 0 and end with a '0' will have 2, 5 and 10 as their factors.
  - Factors are always whole numbers or integers.
- **p. 3 (PDF p. 6):**
  - A number has unlimited multiples.
  - Every number is a multiple of itself.
  - Every multiple of a given number is greater than or equal to the given number.

### Remember Boxes
- **p. 2 (PDF p. 5):** Stop finding factors when the numbers start repeating.
- **p. 4 (PDF p. 7):** To find the multiples of any given number simply multiply the given number by $1, 2, 3, 4, 5, \dots$
- **p. 7 (PDF p. 10):** Any composite number can be written as a product of all of its prime factors.
- **p. 8 (PDF p. 11):** Diagram labeling `Base` ($2$) and `Index` ($5$) in $2^5$.
- **p. 8 (PDF p. 11):** $2 \times 2 \times 3 \times 3 \times 3$ is expanded form and $2^2 \times 3^3$ is index notation.
- **p. 9 (PDF p. 12):** The H.C.F is always smaller than or equal to the smallest number in the set of given numbers.
- **p. 10 (PDF p. 13):** H.C.F (Highest Common Factor) is also called G.C.D (Greatest Common Divisor).
- **p. 11 (PDF p. 14):** If the H.C.F of two or more numbers is 1, then the numbers are called co-prime.
- **p. 13 (PDF p. 16):**
  - If one number is a factor of the other then L.C.M is the greater number.
  - If the given numbers are prime numbers then L.C.M is the product of these numbers.
- **p. 13 (PDF p. 16):** If three or more than three numbers are given to find the L.C.M, then consider a factor as common factor if it is common factor of two numbers or more than two numbers.

### Notes
- **p. 2 (PDF p. 5):** Every number greater than 1 has at least two factors. The number 1 and itself.
- **p. 8 (PDF p. 11):** Students should use only one method to find prime factorization. If it is not specified in the problem.
- **p. 16 (PDF p. 19):** A square box/array should be full if we need to count the number as a square number. Here 12 apples are arranged in a square, but it does not full a square. So, 12 is not a square number.

### Method Rules (HCF & LCM)
- **HCF by Prime Factorization (p. 10 / PDF p. 13):** $\text{H.C.F} = \text{Product of common prime factors of all given numbers}$.
- **HCF by Long Division (pp. 10–11 / PDF pp. 13–14):**
  1. Divide the largest number by the smallest number.
  2. Take remainder as divisor and divide the first divisor by it.
  3. Remainder becomes divisor and second divisor becomes dividend; repeat until remainder is $0$.
  4. Last divisor is H.C.F. For three numbers, find H.C.F of two numbers first, then find H.C.F of that result with the remaining number.
- **LCM by Prime Factorization (p. 13 / PDF p. 16):** $\text{L.C.M} = \text{Product of common two or more prime factors} \times \text{Product of non-common two or more prime factors}$.
- **LCM by Division Method (p. 14 / PDF p. 17):**
  - Step I: Write given numbers in a horizontal line separated by commas.
  - Step II: Choose smallest possible prime number dividing at least one number.
  - Step III: Write quotient directly underneath; bring down undivided numbers.
  - Step IV: Repeat with smallest prime dividing at least one number in the new row.
  - Step V: Continue until all co-prime numbers are left in the last row (in Example 20, continues to $1, 1, 1$).
  - Step VI: Multiply all prime divisors (and any co-prime numbers left in the last row).

Who am I?" *(Multiple valid answers: e.g., $3^2 \times 7 = 63$, $2^2 \times 17 = 68$, $5^2 \times 3 = 75$, $2^2 \times 19 = 76$.)*

### Chapter Summary (p. 18 / PDF p. 21)
1. A factor is a number which divides the dividend completely leaving no remainder.
2. Multiple of a given number is the dividend, for which the given number is its factor.
3. A natural number has only two different factors 1 and the number itself, is called a prime number.
4. A number which has more than two different factors, is called a composite number.
5. The process of factorizing a number into its prime factors is known *(printed "know")* as prime factorization.
6. H.C.F of two or more numbers is a highest number which divides all the given numbers.
7. L.C.M is a least number which is divisible by all the given numbers.

## 9. Flagged Items for Verification (Textbook Typos & Mathematical Discrepancies)

The following items appear verbatim in the textbook or its p. 182 Answer Key and should be verified before authoring automated answer-checking logic:

1. **Example 1(i) (p. 1 / PDF p. 4):** Asks whether `3` is a factor of `42`, computes $42 \div 3 = 14$ with remainder $0$, and concludes `"so, 14 is factor of 42"` instead of `"3 is factor of 42"`.
2. **SLO vs. Section Scope (pp. 1, 4, 7, 12, 16):** SLOs list *"Multiples of numbers up to 2-digit"*, whereas Section 1.3 states *"up to 4-digit"*, Section 1.5 states *"up to 4-digit"*, Section 1.6 states *"two or three numbers up to 3-digit"* (yet Ex. 1.3 Q2(iii) uses `3996` and Q2(vi) uses 4 numbers; Ex. 1.4 Q2(v) uses 4 numbers).
3. **Interesting Information & Review Ex. 1 Q1(v) (pp. 5, 17):** States *"A composite number can [always] be expressed as a sum of two prime numbers"* (Goldbach's conjecture applies to even integers $> 2$, not all odd composite numbers such as 27 or 35).
4. **Sieve of Eratosthenes Step 6 (p. 6 / PDF p. 9):** Prints *"All the numbers left shaded are prime numbers"*; context and steps 2–5 show it means numbers left **unshaded**.
5. **Exercise 1.1 Q7 Answer Key (p. 6 / p. 182):** Question asks for *"neither prime nor composite natural and whole numbers"*; key gives `1` only (excluding `0` from whole numbers).
6. **Thinking Time (p. 7 / PDF p. 10):** *"Which prime number cannot be doubled from the numbers in the 1-10 group?"* — phrasing is unclear (likely refers to $7$, since $2\times7=14$ leaves the $1$–$10$ group, whereas $2, 3, 5$ double to $4, 6, 10$).
7. **Exercise 1.2 Answer Key Typos (p. 182 / PDF p. 185):**
   - Q2(i): key prints $5^2 \times 7$ (textbook question has four 5s: $5^4 \times 7$).
   - Q2(iv): key prints $11^4 \times 13^2 \times 19^3$ (question has one 13: $11^4 \times 13 \times 19^3$).
   - Q4(x): 7875 is omitted from the answer key.
   - Q7: key gives `70` ($2\times5\times7$, 3 primes) as product of most primes below 100, though $2^6=64$ (6 prime factors) or $2\times3\times5=30$ (also 3 distinct primes) exist.
   - Q8(ii): key expands $3^5 \times 5^4 \times 11^3$ with `7`s instead of `5`s.
8. **Example 14 Labels (p. 10 / PDF p. 13):** Third factorization prints `So, 27 = 3 × 3 × 5 × 7` (should be `315`) and arrow box reads `common to 27, 54, and 63` (should be `315`).
9. **Exercise 1.3 Q2(iv) (p. 12 / PDF p. 15):** Part (iv) is mislabeled `(vi)` in the textbook.
10. **Exercise 1.4 Q5 & Q6 Answer Key Errors (p. 182 / PDF p. 185):**
    - Q5: $\text{LCM}(29, 58, 116) = 116$, but key prints `3364` ($29 \times 116$).
    - Q6: $\text{LCM}(30\text{ s}, 60\text{ s}, 90\text{ s}, 120\text{ s}) = 360\text{ s} = 6\text{ minutes}$, so starting at 9:00 a.m. gives `9:06 a.m.`, whereas key prints `09:00 pm or 21:00`.
11. **Section 1.7 Challenge (p. 16 / PDF p. 19):** States $\text{HCF}=10, \text{LCM}=399$, one number $=190$. Mathematically impossible since HCF must divide LCM ($10 \nmid 399$).
12. **Exercise 1.6 Q1 (p. 17 / p. 182):** Parts (iii), (iv), and (v) are all printed as `17`, yet the answer key marks `(v)` as a square number.
13. **Review Exercise 1 Q1(vii) & (viii) Answer Key (p. 17 / p. 182):**
    - Q1(vii): *"The product of factors of a given number is always equal to"* — key gives `a. prime number` (actually product of *prime* factors equals `c. given number`).
    - Q1(viii): *"In $5^4$"* — key gives `c. 4 and 5 are powers`, whereas textbook p. 8 explicitly defines `d. 5 is base and 4 is power`.
14. **Review Exercise 1 Q3 & Q5 Wording (p. 18 / PDF p. 21):** Q3 asks for *"minimum number of participants who can sit in a row"* (answer `12` is HCF, i.e., maximum equal participants per row); Q5 asks for *"largest number which when divided by 24, 36 and 60 leaves 3 as remainder"* (answer `363` is $\text{LCM} + 3$).
